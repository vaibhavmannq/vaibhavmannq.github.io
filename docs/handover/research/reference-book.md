# The reference book: how each thing we make looks and behaves in the real world (D24)

Owner, s08: "real reference for everything that we create so it doesn't look out of place, whether it's the
waterfall, fog, aurora etc. ... and how they would react in a real world scenario so it doesn't feel foreign".

**The rule.** Before any element is built or tuned, open its entry here. Build its look from the named real thing
and its motion from the stated behaviour and numbers. When an entry is thin, do the research first and add to it.
A constant in the code that drives motion or colour should be traceable to a line here.

**Marks.** **[search]** from a source found in s08 (links at the end). **[mine]** my own knowledge of physics or
nature, no source opened: reliable in outline, check a number before leaning on it. I looked at no photo or film;
the owner's eyes close that gap (watch lists in `m2-references.md` and `reference-landforms.md`).

**Each entry ends with "gives it away":** the things that make a made-up version look foreign.

## 1. The clock of the night (light)

- **Golden hour:** sun 0–6° above the horizon. **Civil twilight:** 0 to −6° (the horizon and colours still clear).
  **Blue hour:** about −4 to −8°. **Nautical twilight:** −6 to −12° (horizon still visible at sea, bright stars
  out). **Astronomical twilight:** −12 to −18° ("almost indistinguishable from night"). Night: below −18° [search].
- Our nine hours should each sit at a real sun or moon angle: Golden +4°, Sunset 0°, Dusk −3°, Blue hour −6°,
  Night and Midnight below −18°, Before dawn −10°, First light −4°, Sunrise +1° **[mine, a proposal]**.
- The sun moves 15° an hour; near the horizon its colour goes yellow, orange, red as it drops [mine].
- Opposite the low sun: the Earth's shadow (blue-grey) with a pink band above it [search, in `inspiration-and-ideas.md`].
- **Stars** come out brightest first; they twinkle more near the horizon, and planets hardly at all [mine].
  Thousands are visible only when the sky is fully dark and the moon is down or low.
- **Moonlight** is sunlight reflected: slightly warm in fact, but the dark-adapted eye sees it blue-silver and
  loses colour in everything it lights (the Purkinje shift) [mine]. A full moon lights a landscape about
  400 000× dimmer than the sun; shadows are sharp.
- **Gives it away:** stars at full strength in a sky that is still bright; a moon and an aurora both at full
  power (a bright moon washes an aurora out); shadows pointing the wrong way for where the sun or moon is;
  the light's colour not matching the sky's.

## 2. Wind (everything that moves shares one wind)

Beaufort scale [search]:
| Force | Speed | On land | On water |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 calm | < 1 km/h | smoke rises straight up | "like a mirror" |
| 1 light air | 1–5 km/h | smoke drifts | ripples "with the appearance of scales", no crests |
| 2 light breeze | 6–11 km/h | wind felt on the face, leaves rustle | small wavelets, glassy crests that do not break |
| 3 gentle breeze | 12–19 km/h | leaves and small twigs in constant motion | large wavelets, crests begin to break |
| 4 moderate | 20–28 km/h | small branches move | small waves, some white caps |

- Our mirror-water places (Golden, Blue hour, First light, the lagoon) are **force 0–1**. So grass there may only
  tremble at the tips, and mist must hang, not stream. A waving meadow over a mirror sea is foreign.
- Sunset's meadow and Midnight's ridge can be **force 2–3**: then the sea beyond the meadow shows wavelets, and
  the ridge's tree moves in its twigs, not its trunk.
- Wind comes in gusts: a wave of bending crosses a meadow at the wind's speed (a few metres a second); grass
  bends away, then springs back faster than it bent [mine].
- **One wind per place** (direction and force in the hour preset), read by grass, leaves, petals, mist, the
  cloud deck's drift, lantern drift, ripples and sound. **Gives it away:** things moving in different directions.

## 3. Water

**Still water and ripples.**
- Ripples shorter than 1.7 cm are held by surface tension, longer ones by gravity; nothing on water travels
  slower than 23 cm/s [search]. So a ring from a drop spreads at about 0.2–0.3 m/s, fine ripples running ahead
  of the main ring, and fades within a few metres [mine from the above].
- A mirror needs force 0–1 wind. The mirror image is always a little darker than the thing, and stretched
  vertically by any ripple; at a low angle the water mirrors, straight down you see into it (Fresnel) [mine].
- **Sun and moon roads** are thousands of separate glints from wave facets; calmer water gives a narrower road
  [mine]. Technique in `design-review-and-proposals.md` §3.4.
- Wet sand mirrors only while a thin film of water stands on it, just after a wave draws back; it dulls in
  seconds as it drains [mine].

**Waterfall.**
- Free fall: after a drop of h metres water moves at √(2·9.8·h): 10 m → 14 m/s, 20 m → 20 m/s, 30 m → 24 m/s.
  It **accelerates** all the way; streaks stretch as they fall [mine, school physics].
- The sheet is glassy and clear at the lip, turns white within a few metres as it breaks and takes in air,
  and arrives as ragged clumps and spray. Falls pulse: surges of thicker water follow each other down.
- Mist rises from the foot and drifts with the wind; rock around it is wet, dark and mossy. A plunge pool
  boils white where the water lands, with rings and foam lines running outward.
- At night it is as bright as the sky lighting it, no brighter. Real falls: Gljúfrabúi, Svartifoss,
  Litlanesfoss (`reference-landforms.md`).
- **Gives it away:** constant falling speed; a sheet white from lip to pool; a clean rectangle; no wet rock;
  glowing in the dark.

**Sea.** Swell arrives in sets and wraps round stacks; where it meets rock there is a dark wet band, a white
line of foam that comes and goes, and (see `reference-landforms.md`) a notch. **Gives it away:** rock standing
in water with no meeting line.

## 4. Mist and fog

- **Radiation fog** forms on calm, clear nights as the ground cools; it "usually remains in place", lies from a
  few feet deep up to hundreds, and "tends to dissipate soon after sunrise as the ground warms" [search].
- **Valley fog:** cold air sinks and pools in low ground; this is the **sea of cloud** seen from above [search].
- **Sea smoke** (steam fog): cold air over warmer water; thin wisps rising and vanishing [search].
- **Advection fog** is the kind that moves sideways with the wind [search].
- So: First light's mist is radiation fog or sea smoke: a flat-topped layer a metre or two deep, almost still,
  thinning as the sun arrives at Sunrise. It never streams. The far shore's tops stand clear above it.
- Midnight's cloud sea is valley fog: flat on the large scale, slow, lapping at slopes, **fills only low ground**.
- Fog takes the colour of the light on it: pink at first light, silver under the moon [mine].
- **Gives it away:** fog as an even grey wash; fog moving fast on a calm night; fog with a hard edge; a cloud
  sea that sits at different heights in connected valleys.

## 5. Aurora

- **Colours by height** [search]: green from oxygen (557.7 nm), roughly 100–180 km up, the commonest; red from
  oxygen (630 nm) highest, above about 200–300 km; blue and purple from nitrogen "typically at the lower edges
  of the curtains" and in strong activity; pink is a mix.
- **Shape:** curtains hanging along the Earth's magnetic field, hundreds of kilometres long and very thin; the
  lower edge is the sharp one, the top fades out [mine]. Seen from underneath, the rays converge to one point:
  a corona [search, in `design-review-and-proposals.md`].
- **Motion:** quiet arcs drift slowly; active curtains ripple and fold along their length, rays flicker
  sideways, faster than film can follow [search, `m2-references.md`]. **Pulsating aurora:** soft patches that
  brighten and dim "with periods ranging from a few seconds to tens of seconds", usually late in the night
  [search]: right for Deep midnight.
- It lights the land faintly green and mirrors in still water. Stars shine through it. A bright moon dims it.
- **Gives it away:** a hard top edge; even brightness; a regular comb of rays; one flat band parallel to the
  horizon; nothing on the ground taking its colour.

## 6. Living lights

**Fireflies** [search]: a male of the common North American kind gives one flash of about ¾ second every 5–6
seconds (5.9 ± 0.6 s measured), while flying; each kind has its own code; true synchrony is rare in most kinds.
[mine]: yellow-green; they fly low, a metre or so over grass, most active in the half hour after dusk; the
flash is often made on a rising swoop. So: dark most of the time, each on its own clock, a few dozen at once.
**Gives it away:** lights that stay on; all blinking together; drifting like dust.

**Sea sparkle (plankton)** [search]: a cell flashes only when the water around it is sheared or struck (a wave
breaking, a foot, "an air bubble popping"); the flash starts within 20 ms and lasts about 0.2 s; cells tire
with repeated stirring and recover when left alone; they flash only at night (a body clock), and the light is
blue. So: nothing in still water; sparks exactly where water moves, each gone in a fifth of a second, a
disturbed patch dimmer if stirred again at once. **Gives it away:** a steady glow in calm water; green or
cyan sheets (D2 already forbids them).

**Glowing fungi and glow-worms** [search, `m2-references.md`; mine]: steady, faint, blue-green, never blinking;
they need long exposures to photograph, so they light nothing around them. Fungi glow from caps and gills, in
clusters on rotting wood and damp walls. **Gives it away:** fungi as lamps that light the rock.

## 7. Lanterns and flame

- A flame flickers at about **10 Hz** when it flickers at all (measured on small bundles of candles: 9.9–10.1 Hz;
  the rate depends on gravity and flame size) [search]. A single sheltered candle is nearly steady and only
  shivers when air moves [mine].
- Candle light is deep orange (about 1 900 K) [mine]. Through paper it is a soft even glow, brightest low down
  where the flame is.
- On water a lantern drifts with wind and current at walking pace or less, turns slowly, and bobs with each
  ripple; lanterns launched together spread into a loose line, not a grid [mine]. Each has a warm streak
  below it on the water.
- **Gives it away:** fast random flicker; white or yellow light; lanterns keeping station; no reflection.

## 8. Plants

- Grass: see wind. Blades are lit **through** when the sun is behind them (bright yellow-green edges) and look
  dull and darker front-lit [mine]. Dew at sunrise: points of light toward the sun.
- Trees on an exposed coast are low, lean away from the sea wind, and are thick with twigs (wind-pruned)
  [mine]. Leaves show their paler undersides in gusts.
- Sea pink (thrift), our pink flower, is a real plant of exactly this kind of coast: low cushions of narrow
  leaves on rock ledges and cliff tops, pink heads on thin stalks about 10–20 cm, nodding in wind [mine].
- Heather: a knee-high mat, purple in late summer; moss and fern in every wet shaded place.
- **Gives it away:** plants evenly spaced; the same plant in sun, shade, spray and ridge-top; upright trees
  on a windy headland; flowers that do not move with the grass beside them.

## 9. Rock and ground

See `reference-landforms.md`: one basalt coast, the stack sequence, notches, tide bands, fallen column drums,
plants only on top. Wet rock is darker and shines; dry rock is matt.

## 10. Creatures

- Gulls glide more than they flap, wheel in slow circles on rising air at cliffs, and are seen as pale shapes
  that go dark against a bright sky [mine]. Bats at dusk fly fast with sudden turns. A flock leaving a roost
  at sunrise goes up together and streams one way.
- A fish rising makes one ring (see ripples) and no splash.

## 11. Sound (for D23) [mine]

Sound should obey the same world: calm water laps, it does not crash; a 30 m fall is a steady roar that grows
with nearness and fills an enclosed bowl; a cave adds echo and single drips; the dawn chorus starts with one or
two birds before first light and swells until sunrise; wind is heard on the ridge and in grass, hardly at all
in the calm cove. The loudness of each should follow the place's wind force and the camera's distance.

## How this is used in the work

1. **A reference line in every plan task** that makes or tunes an element: the entry here, the real place or
   film, and the numbers taken from it.
2. **One world state per place** in the hour preset: sun or moon angle, wind direction and force, moon phase.
   Every moving part reads it. This single step removes most "foreign" feelings, because things then agree.
3. **Judging:** each look check asks two questions: does it look like the reference, and does it **behave**
   like it (speed, rhythm, what it responds to). A % for each.
4. **When something reacts to the visitor,** it reacts as the real thing would: plankton to stirred water,
   grass to something passing, water to a touch (a ring at 0.2–0.3 m/s), fireflies not at all to a still
   visitor.

## What our current build gets wrong by these rules (to fix in the milestones)

- Midnight shows a bright moon and a full aurora together, and Night a full aurora over a fall lit white.
- The waterfall's speed is roughly constant in the render; the plan's speeds (9 and 14 m/s) are right at the
  top of a 30 m fall and too slow at the bottom (24 m/s).
- Grass waves over mirror-calm water at Golden and Sunset.
- The plankton glows steadily in still water.
- The fungi are bright enough to read as lamps.
- The cloud sea is planned to drift at 0.6–1.1 m/s: fine for a light breeze, but then the ridge's grass and
  tree must show the same breeze.
- Aurora rays are a regular comb with a hard lower line in the stills.
